  1. If AI Is the Calculator of Our Era, What Is the New “Math”?S. EkinTechRxiv Preprints, 2025

    When calculators entered classrooms, they did not eliminate mathematics-they transformed it. By automating arithmetic, they freed students to focus on higher-order concepts such as algebra, calculus, and modeling. Today, artificial intelligence (AI) marks a similar inflection point: the "cognitive calculator" of the twenty-first century, automating aspects of reasoning itself. This paper asks: if AI is the calculator of our era, what is the new "math" we must teach and learn? We argue that the New Math is not a branch of mathematics but a constellation of literacies and mindsets that prepare humans to collaborate effectively and responsibly with AI. We propose a framework of seven pillars: (1) AI literacy, (2) data literacy, (3) critical thinking and verification, (4) problem framing, (5) creativity and innovation, (6) ethics and responsibility, and (7) collaboration and communication. Drawing on historical analogies, global policy imperatives, and case studies from education, healthcare, business, and civic life, we contend that these literacies must become universal competencies. The New Math is not enrichment but foundational for informed citizenship, economic participation, and democratic resilience. Neglect risks over-reliance, inequity, and erosion of trust; embracing it enables innovation, equity, and human-AI synergy. We conclude that just as past generations committed to universal literacy and numeracy, ours must commit to universal AI literacy and its companion pillars.

    The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) has led to the deployment of interconnected AI systems in urban environments. While these systems promise improved efficiency and convenience, they also introduce risks of emergent behavior, privacy violations, and large-scale manipulation. This paper explores the potential consequences of hierarchical AI systems in a simulated city environment. We model a scenario where citizens rely on personal AI assistants, which are in turn managed by higher-level AI systems responsible for city-wide optimization.     Location determination is an essential component in large sensor networks. In those types of networks, sensors are data centric, and hence, the location of each sensor is critical. Furthermore, the need for a cost-effective means for sensor localization eliminates a variety of options including GPS. In this paper, we use particle filters for estimating the location of sensors assuming a noisy Rayleigh fading channel. Simulation results show that the algorithm converges to approximately two-meter error range.
